# Physics of Ball–Pocket Interactions in Pool and Related Cue Sports

## Executive summary

Pocket behavior is not a single “pocketed vs not” event; it is a **hybrid dynamical system** in which a ball transitions through **geometric gates** (mouth, shelf/fall line, throat) while undergoing **rapid, frictional multi-contact** with pocket points (jaw facings), short rails near the pocket, slate bevels, and pocket liners—under gravity. Official specifications constrain the most load‑bearing geometric variables (mouth width, shelf depth, cut angles, vertical pocket angle/back draft, facing thickness/materials), and experiments/technical measurements show that **approach angle, speed, and spin** strongly change the effective acceptance region: slower “pocket speed” shots can be accepted at angles/situations where faster shots rattle out. citeturn2search0turn2search1turn9view0turn9view1

A simulation that aims to reproduce **bounce in/out, rim hangs, rattles, jams, grazes, lip-scrapes, liner rebounds, and partial entry/escape** needs three things:

- **A faithful geometric model** of each pocket: mouth line/plane, shelf (fall line where the slate bevel begins), jaw/facing surfaces, vertical pocket angle, and liner/boot surfaces. WPA publishes recommended pocket dimensions and liner requirements; BCA specs provide alternative ranges and measurement definitions (including shelf measurement). citeturn2search0turn2search1  
- **A contact solver that handles multi-contact sequences** robustly (jaw–jaw–jaw “rattle,” simultaneous dual-jaw “jam”), either as (A) **event-driven TOI + impulses** or (B) a **compliant contact model** (Hertz-like normal + tangential compliance capped by Coulomb friction). Multi-impact modeling is a known numerical difficulty; state-transition approaches and smooth multi-impact models illustrate why naive sequential impulses can be unstable in tight geometries like pockets. citeturn1search22turn3search23turn10search0  
- **Spin-aware tangential coupling** at every pocket contact: the tangential relative velocity at the contact point depends on both translation and rotation; the resulting friction impulse/force can either **steer the ball into the pocket** (spin-assisted pocketing) or **kick it out** (lip-scrape rebound, rattle-out). citeturn10search0turn3search1turn9view0  

Unspecified elements (exact table model, pocket template, cloth and cleanliness condition) are treated as **no specific constraint**; the report therefore provides **default ranges** and emphasizes **calibration protocols** (high-speed video + trajectory/spin reconstruction) to fit pocket-specific parameters. citeturn2search0turn9view1turn3search6

## Pocket geometry and representations

Pocketing outcomes are geometry-dominated. The same physics (ball mass, cloth friction) will feel dramatically different if the pocket has a deeper shelf or different jaw angles, because the ball must travel farther inside the jaws before gravity can “win” and drop the ball. WPA explicitly defines pocket-relevant dimensions and components (mouth sizes, shelf ranges, cut angles, vertical pocket angle, facings thickness, and liner/boot requirements). citeturn2search0turn2search1

### Geometry primitives you should explicitly model

**Mouth line and mouth plane (gate 1).**  
Specs define “mouth” as the opening between cushion noses where the rail changes direction into the pocket; both WPA and BCA provide mouth size ranges (corner vs side differ). citeturn2search0turn2search1  
Implementation: represent the mouth opening in 2D (table plane) as a polygonal/curvilinear aperture. For state logic, define a **mouth plane** as the vertical extrusion of the mouth line; it distinguishes “outside pocket” vs “entered mouth region.”

**Shelf / fall line / slate-lip edge (gate 2).**  
WPA defines *shelf* as a distance from the mouth line to the *vertical cut of the slate pocket cut* (shelf includes bevel) and provides shelf ranges for corner and side pockets; BCA defines shelf measurement similarly (with slightly different ranges in their equipment document). citeturn2search0turn2search1  
Implementation: represent a second inward boundary—the **shelf boundary**—as a curve/polygon in the table plane (or as the vertical projection of the slate-lip edge). A ball is not physically “committed” until its center passes a geometry-dependent point where gravity and available support contacts force descent; the shelf boundary is a practical approximation to that commitment line. citeturn2search0turn9view2

**Jaw/facing surfaces (dominant rebound & rattle site).**  
WPA requires pocket jaw facings of reinforced rubber; facings must be symmetric left/right, within thickness limits, and “somewhat harder” than the cushions. This matters because facing stiffness and thickness strongly affect how much energy is absorbed vs returned on a jaw hit—central to rattle-outs and lip bounces. citeturn2search0turn2search8

**Vertical pocket angle / back draft (controls “spit back” tendency).**  
Both WPA and BCA specify vertical pocket angles (12°–15°). Larger back draft tends to guide balls downward rather than reflect them back toward the bed. citeturn2search0turn2search1

**Liners and boots (dissipative funnel).**  
WPA specifies that liners/boots should be plastic, rubber, or leather; should not stain balls/cues; the upper inner wall must be shaped so that when struck below the rim it directs a ball downward; and ball returns should not bounce balls back onto the table. These requirements imply that inside-pocket surfaces are intentionally “energy-killing” compared with cushions. citeturn2search0

### Representing pocket geometry in a physics engine

**Analytic surfaces (planes/cylinders/fillets/cones).**  
Pros: fast contact queries; exact normals; stable TOI for sphere–plane/cylinder/cone.  
Cons: hard to match real pocket shapes (jaw rounding, liners) unless you build a careful parametric model.

**Triangle meshes.**  
Pros: matches scanned/CAD pocket shapes; easy authoring.  
Cons: TOI against moving spheres is harder; collision normals can be noisy; requires robust CCD and contact manifold building to avoid “triangle snagging.” Impulse-based methods are sensitive to poor normals in tight pockets. citeturn10search0turn10search1

**Signed-distance fields (SDFs) / distance queries (recommended when pockets are complex).**  
Pros: smooth normals if you use a well-constructed SDF; works with arbitrary shapes; can support conservative advancement.  
Cons: higher compute/memory; building a high-quality SDF for thin rails/lips requires care.

A practical approach used in at least one physically-based billiards simulation workflow is to approximate pocket-adjacent rails as a **set of line segments** (in 2D) and perform collision calculations on the **ball center trajectory** with a radius offset (Minkowski sum idea). This is especially useful near pockets where the rail is not a single straight boundary. citeturn4view0

## Classification of pocket-interaction modes with implementable criteria

The following taxonomy is organized by a **state-machine view**: a ball moves from free motion on cloth into mouth proximity, then possibly into partial entry, then either captures (descends) or escapes (returns). The individual “modes” are different **contact sequences** and **energy/spin states** within that funnel. Multi-contact in a pocket is conceptually similar to multi-point impacts studied in rigid-body impact theory; the difference is that pocket geometry makes rapid alternations common. citeturn1search22turn10search0

### Common detection primitives (used in every mode)

Let the ball center be \(\mathbf{x}\), radius \(R\), linear velocity \(\mathbf{v}\), angular velocity \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\). For any pocket surface \(S\) with outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) at closest point \(\mathbf{p}\):

- **Signed distance** (sphere to surface): \(\phi = d(\mathbf{x},S) - R\). Contact when \(\phi \le \varepsilon_p\).  
- **Normal relative velocity** at contact point:  
  \[
  v_n = \left(\mathbf{v} + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{x})\right)\cdot \mathbf{n}
  \]
- **Tangential slip velocity** at contact:
  \[
  \mathbf{v}_t = \left(\mathbf{v} + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{x})\right) - v_n\mathbf{n}
  \]
These are standard rigid-body contact kinematics for impulse-based simulation. citeturn10search0turn10search1

**Geometric gates (2D, in the table plane)**  
Define two closed regions per pocket:

- \(A_{\text{mouth}}\): mouth aperture region in the table plane.  
- \(A_{\text{shelf}}\): shelf/fall-line aperture region (typically “deeper” and often narrower in effective clearance).

Then for a sphere we use a dilation by radius \(R\): a center is “inside” if it lies within the **configuration-space aperture** \(A \ominus R\) (equivalently, check whether the 2D distance from the center to the aperture boundary exceeds \(R\)). This corresponds to the common “offset the boundary by one radius” trick described in event-based billiards simulation settings. citeturn4view0turn2search0

## Collision/contact response in pockets and derived equations for cavity dynamics

Pocket dynamics differs from free-table motion mainly because of (i) **gravity-driven descent**, (ii) frequent **multi-surface contact**, and (iii) deliberately dissipative interior materials (liners/boots). WPA requirements about liner shaping and preventing ball return “bounce-outs” motivate choosing **low restitution + high damping** for liner contacts compared with rails. citeturn2search0

### Equations of motion inside the pocket cavity

Treat the ball as a rigid sphere under gravity and contact forces:

\[
m\dot{\mathbf{v}} = m\mathbf{g} + \sum_k \mathbf{F}_{c,k}, \qquad
I\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \sum_k (\mathbf{r}_k \times \mathbf{F}_{c,k})
\]

with \(I=\tfrac{2}{5}mR^2\) for a homogeneous sphere. citeturn10search0

Inside a pocket there can be multiple simultaneous contacts \(k\) with jaw faces, bevel surfaces, liners, and the pocket bottom. Multi-contact handling is a central issue in rigid-body simulation; iterative constraint solvers and impact-space state-transition formulations are common ways to avoid instability. citeturn10search1turn1search22turn3search23

### Impulse-based contact (fast, practical)

For each contact with normal \(\mathbf{n}\), contact point \(\mathbf{p}\), define \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{x}\) and relative contact velocity \(\mathbf{v}_c=\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}\).

**Normal impulse** (Newton restitution):
\[
J_n = -(1+e)\,\frac{v_n}{\frac{1}{m} + \mathbf{n}\cdot\left(I^{-1}(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{n})\times\mathbf{r}\right)}
\quad\text{for } v_n<0
\]
**Tangential friction impulse** (Coulomb cap):
\[
\mathbf{t}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{\|\mathbf{v}_t\|+\varepsilon_t},\qquad
J_t^\* = -\frac{\mathbf{v}_t\cdot\mathbf{t}}{\frac{1}{m} + \mathbf{t}\cdot\left(I^{-1}(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{t})\times\mathbf{r}\right)}
\]
\[
J_t = \mathrm{clamp}\left(J_t^\*, -\mu |J_n|, +\mu |J_n|\right)
\]
Update:
\[
\mathbf{v}^+ = \mathbf{v}^- + \frac{J_n\mathbf{n}+J_t\mathbf{t}}{m},\qquad
\boldsymbol{\omega}^+ = \boldsymbol{\omega}^- + I^{-1}\left(\mathbf{r}\times(J_n\mathbf{n}+J_t\mathbf{t})\right)
\]

These are standard impulse-based rigid-body collision formulas; using a robust contact solver (or iterating impulses across all active contacts) helps with the rapid jaw alternations of rattles and the simultaneous contacts of jams. citeturn10search0turn10search1turn1search22

### Compliant/Hertz option (better peak forces and “squish”)

If you want contact duration, peak force, and speed-dependent restitution, replace instantaneous impulses with a force law, commonly with Hertz-type normal stiffness:

\[
F_n = k_n \delta^{3/2} + c_n \delta^{1/2}\dot{\delta}
\]
where \(\delta\) is penetration depth. Hertzian scaling and effective modulus concepts are described in contact mechanics references. citeturn10search2turn10search6

Tangential force can be modeled by a tangential spring-dashpot capped by Coulomb friction:
\[
\mathbf{F}_t = -k_t \boldsymbol{\xi}_t - c_t \mathbf{v}_t,\qquad \|\mathbf{F}_t\|\le \mu F_n
\]
Compliant methods can better reproduce the “softening” effect of facings/liners (reduced spit-backs), but require smaller timesteps for stability, especially in stiff contacts. citeturn10search2turn3search23

### Capture volumes and escape conditions after partial entry

A robust pocketing model avoids “binary” capture at the mouth. Use a **capture volume** \(V_{\text{cap}}\) that sits inside the pocket and has hysteresis:

- **Enter pocket-cavity simulation** when \(\mathbf{x}\) crosses into \(A_{\text{shelf}}\) (gate 2) and the ball’s lowest point \(z-R\) becomes negative beyond a small threshold (e.g., \(z-R < -\varepsilon_z\)). Shelf definitions and ranges are specified by WPA/BCA. citeturn2search0turn2search1  
- **Declare “captured”** only when \(\mathbf{x}\) crosses a deeper boundary such as \(z < z_{\text{lock}}\) (e.g., center is at least one radius below the bed plane) *and* outward escape would require interpenetrating the offset pocket geometry (approximate by checking that the center is outside the offset of \(A_{\text{mouth}}\)). This prevents false captures from brief mouth crossings during rattles.

**Escape after partial entry** (classification; not necessarily a hard rule):  
If \(\mathbf{x}\in A_{\text{mouth}}\) but \(\mathbf{x}\notin A_{\text{shelf}}\), and after the most recent pocket-surface impulse the outward component of velocity satisfies \((\mathbf{v}\cdot \hat{o})>0\) and the next TOI with pocket surfaces exceeds a clearance distance, treat as “escaped” and return to cloth dynamics. This aligns with the shelf’s role as the key geometric difficulty factor. citeturn2search0turn9view2

## Spin and precession effects on pocketing probability

Spin matters in pockets for the same reason it matters in cushions: it changes the **contact-point relative velocity**, altering friction force/impulse direction and magnitude, and therefore changes whether the ball “sticks, slides, climbs, or steers” along a jaw or liner.

### Spin-induced forces and torques at a jaw/liner contact

At a pocket contact point \(\mathbf{p}\) with normal \(\mathbf{n}\):

\[
\mathbf{v}_c = \mathbf{v} + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{x}),\qquad
\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{v}_c - (\mathbf{v}_c\cdot \mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}
\]

A Coulomb friction force \(\mathbf{F}_t = -\mu F_n \frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{\|\mathbf{v}_t\|}\) produces a torque:
\[
\boldsymbol{\tau} = (\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{x})\times \mathbf{F}_t
\]
which changes \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) and can rotate the spin axis (precession-like behavior due to torque perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\)). Impulse-based equivalents use \(\Delta\boldsymbol{\omega}=I^{-1}(\mathbf{r}\times \mathbf{J})\). citeturn10search0turn10search1

**Interpretation for pocket modes (mechanistic):**

- **Topspin (“follow”) aiding pocketing:** on a jaw impact, if the contact point slip points such that friction produces an inward lateral component, the ball can redirect into the throat and lose enough translational energy to drop rather than rebound. In measured pocket-size experiments, softer shots tended to fall more often partly because follow carries the ball to a better second-jaw contact and helps it “over the brink,” while faster shots have less time for that rotational coupling to develop. citeturn9view0  
- **Backspin increasing rattle-out risk:** if backspin increases tangential slip opposite the motion, it can increase the magnitude of friction impulses that redirect outward after a jaw graze (depending on normal direction), promoting escape in marginal cases (this is a model-based inference from the contact kinematics and Coulomb friction; validate with experiments). citeturn10search0turn10search1  
- **Sidespin steering on liners:** when contacting a near-vertical liner wall, \(\omega_z\) produces tangential surface velocity \(R\omega_z\) at the side, which can create a lateral friction force that pulls the ball along the wall deeper into the pocket or, if geometry is unfavorable, pushes it back toward the mouth. This is directly captured by the \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}\) term. citeturn10search0turn2search0  

### Pocket acceptance vs speed/angle and the role of spin

Two experimentally supported qualitative facts are especially important for simulation tuning:

- **Effective pocket size decreases at higher speed**, and high-speed shots are more prone to “rattle out,” especially when the near jaw/rail is grazed first. citeturn9view1turn9view0  
- **Corner and side pockets behave differently with angle**, with side pocket acceptance degrading sharply at large angles. citeturn9view1  

Therefore, if you are building an “acceptance chart” (angle vs speed vs spin), expect a surface where acceptance probability falls with speed and with unfavorable angles, and is modulated by spin through the tangential impulse mechanism above. Jewett’s measurements provide a starting qualitative calibration that acceptance for “soft” shots can exceed “hard” shots substantially at certain angles (e.g., around a few degrees off the rail). citeturn9view0turn9view1

## Numerical guidance and avoiding tunneling or false captures

Pocket regions are the most numerically fragile part of billiards simulation: tight clearances, high curvature, and fast event sequences. Techniques from rigid-body simulation practice are therefore essential. citeturn10search0turn10search1turn1search22

### CCD vs event-driven TOI

**Strong recommendation:** use **continuous collision detection (CCD)** or an **event-driven time-of-impact (TOI)** loop for pocket boundaries and jaws.

- At high speeds, discrete stepping can skip over a jaw contact, producing nonphysical “ghost pocketing” or missing a rattle that should reject. CCD is standard advice in impulse-based rigid body simulation and is also emphasized in iterative contact approaches for stable time stepping. citeturn10search0turn10search1  
- If you use event-driven TOI: after each event, recompute TOIs because the velocity direction changes; this is critical for double-hits near pockets and multi-jaw rattles. Event-list approaches are used in billiards simulation frameworks that predict collision times analytically between events. citeturn4view0

### Contact tolerances and “capture hysteresis”

Suggested engineering tolerances (in meters, assuming meter units):

- Contact distance slop: \(\varepsilon_p \sim 10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-4}\) m (10–100 µm) for pocket surfaces; larger values can create false jams/hangs; smaller values can cause jitter if normals are noisy (mesh). (This is an engineering recommendation; validate against your scale and floating-point precision.) citeturn10search1turn10search0  
- Velocity thresholds: \(v_{\text{sleep}} \sim 10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-2}\) m/s for “resting in pocket,” but only after ensuring the ball is in a stable contact configuration; otherwise you can incorrectly freeze a ball mid-rattle. citeturn10search1turn1search22  
- Capture hysteresis: use two boundaries, \(A_{\text{shelf}}\) to enter pocket-cavity simulation and a deeper \(V_{\text{cap}}\) (or \(z_{\text{lock}}\)) to declare irreversible capture; this directly addresses false captures from brief mouth crossings. Shelf definitions and ranges are codified in WPA/BCA specs. citeturn2search0turn2search1  

### Multi-contact stability strategies (rattle and jam)

- If using impulse sequences, iterate over all active contacts at a given time and apply impulses repeatedly (or use a PGS solver) until constraint violations are small; this is aligned with iterative dynamics formulations in real-time engines. citeturn10search1  
- For near-simultaneous dual-jaw impacts (jam), consider treating the set of contacts as one multi-impact “phase.” State-transition models of simultaneous impacts formalize this idea: impacts may become active/inactive as restitution finishes or new penetrations occur. citeturn1search22  
- Smooth multi-impact models have been proposed specifically in a pool/billiards context to represent multiple impacts without the discontinuities of event-driven impulses; these are viable if you can afford smaller timesteps and want smoother trajectories in pocket rattles. citeturn3search23

**Sensitivity guidance (what changes outcomes the most):**

- **Shelf depth and facing geometry** dominate “hang vs drop” and “rattle vs fall” in marginal cases; both WPA/BCA treat shelf as a core dimension. citeturn2search0turn2search1turn9view2  
- **Shot speed** strongly changes effective target size; hard shots can effectively reduce pocket acceptance and increase rattle-outs. citeturn9view1turn9view0  
- **Friction at jaw contacts** controls how much spin influences steering in or out; even modest \(\mu\) changes can flip outcomes for near-point grazes because tangential impulses determine whether the second jaw contact is “friendly.” citeturn10search0turn9view0  

### Validation experiments and data-collection protocols

To fit pocket-specific parameters, focus on experiments that isolate pocket behavior from general cloth dynamics.

**Recommended measurement setup (practical):**

- **Overhead high-speed video** at 240–1000 fps if possible; one study in pool motor learning used high-speed camera tracking for ball trajectories (demonstrating feasibility of kinematic tracking). citeturn3search6  
- Add a **side camera** aimed at the pocket to capture vertical motion, rim contacts, and liner rebounds.  
- Use **fiducial calibration** (table markers) to reconstruct \((x,y)\) accurately and estimate \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) via marked balls or optical flow (if you can). (Spin estimation is harder; treat as an advanced protocol.)

**Pocket-focused experiments (each yields fit targets):**

1. **Acceptance map (angle–speed grid) per pocket type**  
   For each pocket: sweep approach angle and speed; record pocketed vs rejected. Compare to known behavior that effective target size shrinks with speed and with large angles, especially for side pockets. citeturn9view1turn9view0  

2. **Rattle statistics**  
   Deliberately aim near points at different speeds; record number of jaw contacts before outcome and post-rattle exit speed/direction. Fit jaw \(e,\mu\) and facing compliance/damping using observed decay across impacts. citeturn9view1turn1search22  

3. **Hanger stability test**  
   Place balls in marginal resting configurations (by hand or gentle nudges) to determine when they hang vs drop; fit static friction and bevel geometry; validate that your solver can represent stable equilibrium without jitter. (Use constraint solver techniques for resting contact.) citeturn10search1turn2search0  

4. **Liner rebound suppression**  
   Drop/roll balls into the pocket and measure whether any rebound onto the bed occurs; tune liner restitution and damping to align with WPA intent that inner walls direct downward and returns do not bounce balls back onto the table. citeturn2search0  

### Pocket state-transition diagram (for implementation)

```mermaid
stateDiagram-v2
  [*] --> FreeOnCloth

  FreeOnCloth --> MouthProximity: center enters A_mouth (offset)
  MouthProximity --> JawContact: contact with facing/jaw surfaces
  MouthProximity --> FreeOnCloth: leaves A_mouth without contact

  JawContact --> Rattle: alternating jaw hits (N>=2 within Tr)
  Rattle --> PartialEntry: center inside A_mouth, not inside A_shelf
  Rattle --> FreeOnCloth: exits A_mouth outward

  PartialEntry --> ShelfCrossed: center enters A_shelf (offset)
  PartialEntry --> FreeOnCloth: outward crossing + clearance (escape)

  ShelfCrossed --> InCavity: z drops below bed, liner/bevel contact
  InCavity --> Captured: center below z_lock and no geometric escape
  InCavity --> SpitBack: rebound upward/outward to A_mouth
  SpitBack --> FreeOnCloth: exits A_mouth outward

  Captured --> SettledInPocket: v and omega below sleep thresholds
  SettledInPocket --> [*]
```

This state machine matches the specification-driven gates (mouth, shelf) and supports rattle, spit-back, and hanger/jam as emergent subcases (resting contact in JawContact/PartialEntry/InCavity). Shelf definitions and their role in difficulty are emphasized in WPA/BCA and technical discussion of pocket geometry’s impact. citeturn2search0turn2search1turn9view2

### Pseudocode: pocket decision logic with multi-contact pocket dynamics

```pseudo
function step_sim(dt):
  # 1) Predict next event time using CCD / TOI for:
  #    - ball-ball, ball-rail
  #    - ball-pocket surfaces (jaw, bevel, liner)
  t_event, contact_set = compute_next_TOI(dt)

  # 2) Integrate continuous motion to t_event
  integrate_free_motion(t_event)

  # 3) Resolve contacts at event time
  if contact_set not empty:
     if use_impulses:
        # iterate to handle simultaneous/multi-contact (rattle/jam)
        for iter in 1..N_iter:
           for c in contact_set:
              apply_normal_and_friction_impulses(c)
           contact_set = rebuild_active_contacts(eps_p)
           if contacts_converged(contact_set): break
     else:
        # compliant/contact-force mode
        for substep in 1..N_sub:
           compute_contact_forces(contact_set)
           integrate_forces(dt_sub)

  # 4) Update pocket state machine
  for ball in balls:
     update_gate_membership(ball, A_mouth, A_shelf)
     if ball.state == FreeOnCloth and ball.in_mouth: ball.state = MouthProximity
     if ball.state in {MouthProximity, JawContact, Rattle, PartialEntry}:
         if ball.in_shelf: ball.state = ShelfCrossed
         else if escaped_outward(ball): ball.state = FreeOnCloth

     if ball.state == ShelfCrossed:
         ball.state = InCavity  # switch to cavity surfaces + gravity

     if ball.state == InCavity:
         if captured(ball): ball.state = Captured
         else if spit_back(ball): ball.state = SpitBack

     if ball.state == Captured and near_rest(ball):
         ball.state = SettledInPocket
```

The convergence loop for impulses mirrors widely used iterative dynamics approaches for contacts, which are especially important for multi-contact rattles and wedges. citeturn10search1turn1search22turn10search0
